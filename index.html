Pulsed & CW ODMR Control System

Dutt Lab · Quantum Control, Timing Electronics, Inter-Instrument Triggering, Acousto-Optic Modulators, Data Acquisition

A Python pulse-sequencing and calibration stack for NV-center magnetic resonance, reaching 0.8 ns timing resolution across laser and microwave channels. I architected a state-machine ADbasic program for T1, Rabi, Ramsey, and Hahn-echo measurements. The architecture runs on inter-instrument triggering between the AWG and photon counter: AOMs modulate the lasers and an IQ mixer modulates the microwave, both driven by a Proteus AWG. I use the MultiHarp for offline gating of the signal and reference counts, which improves SNR.

Multi-instrument Triggering for Pulsed ODMR

Bringing up and characterizing the multi-instrument triggering that pulsed ODMR depends on. I measured the trigger-to-output latency of a Proteus AWG driving an ADwin — 902 ns with 0.03 ns drift and 2.3 ns jitter, shot-noise limited — separated the fixed AOM acoustic delay from the true 29 ns optical rise, and aligned the readout gate to the optical pulse so the signal and reference windows sample the same light.

Spin-Charge Conversion & the Dye Laser

An alternative NV readout that beats ordinary fluorescence detection: it uses the excited-state intersystem-crossing dynamics to protect one spin state from an intense ionization pulse, leaving a spin-dependent charge distribution that a charge-selective pulse then reads out — for longer measurement times and lower readout noise. I aligned and tuned the dye ring-laser cavity, wrote the Python client for the spectrometer, wavemeter, and dye-laser wavelength-tuning server, and refurbished the dye and chiller circulators.

Analog / Digital AOM Driver

A tunable hybrid acousto-optic modulator driver that controls a laser through both a TTL switch (on/off) and a voltage-variable attenuator (amplitude), with pulse shaping from an arbitrary waveform generator. I salvaged the bulk components from a dead fixed-frequency driver, added variable VCO control so it works across crystals, rebuilt power distribution around step-up converters and a computer power supply, laid it out on a PCB, and verified it on a real beam with a photodiode. Built for ECE 1895 in a six-week design cycle.

Spectrometer Demonstration Toy

A benchtop grating spectrometer controlled entirely with 555 timers. One astable stage turns a potentiometer setting into the PWM pulse width that positions a servo-mounted diffraction grating to select wavelength; a second, light-dependent stage swings a servo shutter to protect the detector when the incident light gets too bright. I modeled both circuits in LTspice — servos as inductive loads — matched the simulated pulse widths to oscilloscope measurements on the breadboard, then took the design through an Altium schematic, a two-layer PCB, and 3D-printed laser and grating mounts to fix the optical alignment. An individual project for ECE 1895.

Qub-It — a Quantum-Computing Bop-It

A team-built educational spin on Bop-It that makes single-qubit mechanics tangible: a 3D-printed Bloch sphere whose LEDs light along the X, Y, and Z axes to show the qubit's state, driven from a handheld controller with three rotary encoders for the X/Y/Z gates and three basis-measurement buttons. A laser hitting a photodiode initializes the qubit, the shrinking round timer stands in for decoherence, and a measurement collapses the state probabilistically. My part was the system architecture and firmware — the ATmega finite-state machine, the audio and LCD subsystems, and the LED dimming — plus the photodiode initialization, the PCB schematic, and a second board revision. It doubles as an outreach tool for introducing qubits to students.

About

I'm Jannet — an engineering physics major at the University of Pittsburgh, with minors in physics and electrical engineering and certificates in photonics and in quantum computing and information. What pulls me toward experiment is the apparatus itself: I like designing and building the instruments that make the measurements — testing how real physics squares with theory, and turning that physics into new devices. Quantum sensing sits right where optics, electronics, and code meet.

I've been in the Dutt Lab since January 2024, working mostly on NV centers in diamond — pulsed and CW ODMR, confocal microscopy, wide-field imaging, and the FastAPI control layer that lets the whole bench talk to itself. Along the way I've tuned dye lasers, laid out PCBs, run CFD and thermal simulations in SOLIDWORKS, and written a good deal of the software the lab runs on. I'm currently getting data for a first-author manuscript on an adiabatic NV DEER experiment.

Outside the lab, I spent a summer studying engineering at Charles University in Prague and ran quantum-computing outreach events for middle schoolers. I like teaching almost as much as building — writing the SOPs and training guides that get new researchers up to speed is some of the work I'm proudest of.
